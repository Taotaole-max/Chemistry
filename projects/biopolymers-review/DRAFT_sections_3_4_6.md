## 3. Structure–Property Relationships of the Major Biopolymer Families

This section examines five biopolymer families ordered along a single organising axis: the flexibility of the polymer backbone and the strength of the intermolecular forces it permits. At one extreme sits cellulose, whose extended, hydrogen-bonded backbone locks it into a crystalline solid that never melts; at the other sits natural rubber, whose freely rotating backbone yields an amorphous elastomer that crystallises only under strain. Between these poles lie polysaccharides with weaker or ionically tunable interchain forces (starch, chitin/chitosan, alginate), semicrystalline polyesters whose backbone flexibility permits melt processing but only within narrow thermal windows (PHA, PLA), and template-folded proteins whose properties arise from defined secondary/tertiary structure rather than simple chain packing. Lignin, an irregularly cross-linked aromatic network with no periodic chain at all, closes the section as the structural counterpoint to every other family discussed. The claim tested throughout — introduced in §2.2 — is that backbone chemistry, not biological origin, predicts melting behaviour, hydrolytic/enzymatic susceptibility, and mechanical performance; Table 1 restates this claim quantitatively.

### 3.1 Polysaccharides

Polysaccharides occupy the rigid end of the spectrum, but the family itself spans a wide range depending on how strongly — and how tunably — their chains interact.

**Cellulose.** Cellulose is a linear homopolymer of D-glucopyranose units linked by β-(1→4) glycosidic bonds. Because the β linkage inverts the orientation of each successive residue by 180°, the true repeat unit is cellobiose, and the chain adopts an extended, ribbon-like conformation rather than a helix. This conformation is locked in place by an intramolecular hydrogen bond (O3–H···O5′) that restricts rotation about the glycosidic bond, while a second intramolecular bond (O2–H···O6′) and an extensive network of interchain hydrogen bonds organise adjacent chains into sheets; these sheets stack via van der Waals forces on their hydrophobic faces. The result is one of the most crystalline polymers known in nature, existing in two allomorphs — triclinic Iα (dominant in algae and bacterial cellulose) and monoclinic Iβ (dominant in higher plants) — with metastable Iα converting to the thermodynamically favoured Iβ on annealing. The practical consequence of this hydrogen-bonded architecture is severe: cellulose has no accessible melting transition, decomposing at ~300 °C before any melt could form, and it resists dissolution in ordinary solvents. Processing is therefore restricted to solution routes — NaOH/urea aqueous systems, LiCl/DMAc, NMMO monohydrate (the basis of the Lyocell process), and ionic liquids — all of which work by disrupting the hydrogen-bond network rather than by heating. Native cellulose reaches a degree of polymerisation on the order of 10⁴; regenerative processing (viscose, Lyocell) degrades this to a few hundred. The axial elastic modulus of the crystal, ~130–150 GPa, rivals engineering fibres and is the mechanical signature of the same hydrogen-bonded lattice that prevents melting — a direct illustration of the section's central claim that intermolecular architecture, not monomer identity, sets both the ceiling on stiffness and the floor on processability. Cellulose nanocrystals (CNC, isolated by acid hydrolysis of disordered regions) and cellulose nanofibrils (CNF, isolated mechanically or via TEMPO-mediated oxidation) exploit this crystal modulus as reinforcing fillers in composite and nanopaper applications.

**Starch.** Starch is a mixture of two polysaccharides with the same monomer but different topology: amylose, a largely linear α-(1→4) glucan that adopts a single helix (and can complex guest molecules such as iodine), and amylopectin, a heavily branched α-(1→4) chain with α-(1→6) branch points roughly every 20–25 residues. This topological difference — helix versus branched network — rather than any difference in glycosidic chemistry, explains why starch behaves so differently from cellulose despite sharing the same monomer: branching and helical packing produce weaker, more disruptable crystalline order than cellulose's extended sheets, so starch granules are only semicrystalline (A, B, and C polymorphs distinguished by helix packing density) and lose that order readily on heating in water (gelatinisation), then partially regain it on cooling and storage (retrogradation — the molecular basis of staling). Thermoplastic starch (TPS) exploits this weaker crystallinity by plasticising the granule with glycerol or sorbitol; because water itself acts as a plasticiser, the glass transition temperature of TPS is strongly and reversibly dependent on ambient humidity. This is starch's defining weakness: unlike cellulose's covalently fixed rigidity, TPS properties drift with environmental moisture, which is why TPS is used almost exclusively as a blend component (with PLA or PBAT) rather than as a stand-alone material.

**Chitin and chitosan.** Chitin, a β-(1→4)-linked polymer of N-acetylglucosamine, occurs as antiparallel α-chitin (crustacean shells, the most abundant form) or parallel β-chitin (squid pens). Chitosan is produced by partial deacetylation of chitin, and the degree of deacetylation (DD) is the single variable that governs its behaviour: at DD sufficient to leave a population of free amine groups (pKa ≈ 6.3–6.5), chitosan protonates in dilute acid to become a linear polycation — the source of its solubility in acidic media, its ability to form polyelectrolyte complexes with anionic polymers, and its antimicrobial activity against negatively charged microbial membranes. The deacetylation reaction, however, is not selective: the alkaline conditions required also cleave the backbone, so high-DD chitosan is necessarily lower in molecular weight than low-DD chitosan. This DD–MW trade-off is a genuine processing constraint, not a minor caveat, and is worth stating explicitly as a critical point rather than a footnote.

**Alginate.** Alginate is a block copolymer of β-D-mannuronic acid (M) and α-L-guluronic acid (G), arranged in homopolymeric M-blocks, homopolymeric G-blocks, and alternating MG regions whose proportions vary by algal source. Divalent cations — chiefly Ca²⁺ — cooperatively coordinate GG-block sequences in the "egg-box" arrangement, producing ionotropic gelation at room temperature without any covalent chemistry. Because gel stiffness scales with the extent of GG-block coordination, the G-content of a given alginate source directly and predictably sets the trade-off between gel stiffness and brittleness — a second clean illustration of composition-to-property causality, and one exploited directly in biomedical hydrogels (§7.2).

**Other polysaccharides** — hyaluronic acid, carrageenan, xanthan gum, and pectin — follow the same principle (backbone linkage chemistry and charge state set solution behaviour and gelation mechanism) and are noted briefly for their applications in §7 rather than treated in structural depth here, given the word budget.

### 3.2 Polyesters: PHA and PLA

Moving toward the flexible end of the spectrum, PHA and PLA are semicrystalline aliphatic polyesters: their ester-linked backbones are flexible enough to crystallise into a true melt-processable phase, which polysaccharides cannot do — but this same flexibility narrows their thermal stability, producing the opposite failure mode to cellulose's.

**PHA family.** Polyhydroxyalkanoates are synthesised intracellularly by bacteria as carbon- and energy-storage granules, and are classified by monomer chain length: short-chain-length PHA (scl-PHA, C3–C5 monomers, of which poly(3-hydroxybutyrate), PHB, is the archetype) and medium-chain-length PHA (mcl-PHA, C6–C14, typically from *Pseudomonas* species). PHB itself is highly crystalline (55–70%), with Tm ≈ 175 °C and Tg ≈ 4 °C, and it undergoes secondary crystallisation during storage that embrittles finished parts over time — a well-documented aging failure distinct from any chemical degradation. Its more serious limitation is thermal: PHB decomposes via a six-membered-ring cis-elimination mechanism that produces random chain scission (yielding crotonic acid and oligomeric fragments), and the onset of this decomposition sits only tens of degrees above Tm. The processing window between "cannot yet melt" and "already decomposing" is consequently one of the narrowest of any commercial thermoplastic. The standard countermeasure is copolymerisation — incorporating hydroxyvalerate units (PHBV) or hydroxyhexanoate units (PHBHHx) — which lowers both Tm and crystallinity and widens the window at the cost of stiffness.

**PLA.** Poly(lactic acid) is, strictly, not itself a directly biosynthesised polymer: fermentation supplies the lactic acid monomer, which is cyclised to lactide and then ring-opened by catalysed polymerisation (industrially, Sn(Oct)₂-catalysed ROP) to reach commercially useful molecular weight — direct polycondensation of lactic acid cannot reach high MW because the water by-product cannot be removed efficiently from the increasingly viscous melt. PLA's stereochemistry (the ratio of L-, D-, and meso-lactide) is the dominant property lever: poly(L-lactide) (PLLA) has Tg ≈ 55–60 °C — its practical heat-resistance ceiling — and Tm ≈ 170–180 °C, but crystallises slowly and typically requires nucleating agents or annealing to develop useful crystallinity. A striking exception to this slow-crystallisation limitation is the PLA stereocomplex: a 1:1 blend of PLLA and its enantiomer PDLA co-crystallises into a distinct stereocomplex lattice with Tm ≈ 230 °C, roughly 50 °C above either homopolymer — a direct, quantifiable demonstration that intermolecular packing (here, complementary stereochemical fit) rather than covalent backbone chemistry sets the thermal ceiling.

Briefly, PBS and PEF follow the same aliphatic/furanoate-ester logic; PEF in particular is discussed in §7 as a bio-based analogue to PET with superior gas-barrier properties despite lower crystallinity.

### 3.3 Protein-based Polymers

Proteins occupy a structurally distinct position on the spectrum: their properties arise not from simple chain packing or crystallinity in the polysaccharide/polyester sense, but from a genetically encoded, sequence-specific fold. This is the section's clearest demonstration that "backbone chemistry" must be read broadly — for proteins, the relevant variable is sequence-directed secondary structure, not glycosidic or ester linkage geometry.

**Silk fibroin.** The heavy chain of *Bombyx mori* silk fibroin is dominated by short repeats (Gly-Ala-Gly-Ala-Gly-Ser)ₙ that assemble into stacked antiparallel β-sheet nanocrystallites dispersed within a more compliant amorphous matrix. Silk exists as Silk I (a more helical/disordered state, present in the gland) and Silk II (β-sheet-rich, formed under the shear and pH change of spinning); this liquid-to-solid, disorder-to-order transition during spinning is itself the basis of the fibre's mechanical properties. The combination of rigid nanocrystalline reinforcement within a compliant matrix is what gives silk its unusual simultaneous strength and toughness, a nanocomposite logic more akin to engineered composites than to conventional semicrystalline polymers.

**Collagen and gelatin.** Collagen's defining feature is a triple helix formed by three polypeptide chains with obligatory Gly-X-Y repeats, where X is frequently proline and Y frequently hydroxyproline; the hydroxyl group of hydroxyproline stabilises the triple helix (via bound-water hydrogen bonding), so its enzymatic installation is a prerequisite for helix stability, not a cosmetic modification. Thermal or acidic denaturation converts the ordered triple helix irreversibly into gelatin, a random-coil polymer with entirely different (and tunable, via cross-linking) mechanical and gelling behaviour — a clean example of how loss of a single hierarchical structural level changes macroscopic performance completely.

**Zein, casein, and soy protein.** These globular, less hierarchically structured proteins are used primarily for their film-forming ability in food-contact and coating applications rather than for structural performance, and are noted here for completeness (detail deferred to §7).

**The molecular-weight contrast.** Because protein (and nucleic acid) synthesis is template-directed by the ribosome, chain length and sequence are fixed exactly for every molecule of a given protein: dispersity Đ = 1.0. No synthetic or enzymatically-extended biopolymer achieves this monodispersity (§4) — it is the single sharpest distinction between templated and non-templated biopolymer synthesis, and it is worth flagging here as the bridge into §4 rather than leaving it as an isolated fact in that section.

### 3.4 Lignin and Other Aromatics

Lignin closes the rigid-to-irregular half of the spectrum as its structural counterpoint: where cellulose is rigid *because* it is regular, lignin is amorphous *because* it is irregular. It is built from three monolignol units — p-coumaryl (H), coniferyl (G), and sinapyl (S) alcohols — joined by a heterogeneous mixture of linkages (β-O-4 aryl ether being the most abundant, alongside β-β, β-5, and 5-5 carbon–carbon linkages) formed by radical coupling during biosynthesis rather than by any templated or enzymatically periodic process. The result is a randomly cross-linked, three-dimensional aromatic network with no repeating "chain" in the sense used for every other family in this section, and whose exact linkage distribution varies by plant species, tissue, and — critically for industrial use — by extraction method (kraft, organosolv, and lignosulfonate lignins differ substantially in purity, MW, and reactive functionality). This structural irregularity, more than any inherent lack of chemical value, is the root cause of lignin's persistent difficulty in high-value valorisation: unlike cellulose or PLA, there is no single "lignin" whose structure–property relationship can be stated once.

### 3.5 Natural Rubber

Natural rubber is the spectrum's flexible extreme and, appropriately, its one genuine exception to the "crystallinity governs performance" logic that organises the rest of this section. *cis*-1,4-polyisoprene, harvested at very high molecular weight (Mw often exceeding 10⁶ g mol⁻¹) from *Hevea brasiliensis* latex, is amorphous at rest — its freely rotating, sterically undemanding backbone has none of cellulose's hydrogen-bond lock-in or even PLA's stereochemical packing constraints. Under strain, however, chain alignment triggers strain-induced crystallisation (SIC), generating crystallites that act as physical cross-links and self-reinforce the network precisely where and when mechanical stress demands it — a property essentially absent in synthetic *cis*-polyisoprene, whose lower stereoregularity crystallises far less efficiently under strain. Non-rubber components carried over from the latex (proteins, phospholipids) further participate in network formation and are increasingly understood as active, not incidental, contributors to this self-reinforcing behaviour. Natural rubber therefore demonstrates that the organising variable of this section is not "crystallinity" per se but *intermolecular architecture under the conditions of use* — a claim that folds neatly back into Table 1's structure at the end of §3.

## 4. Molecular Weight and Its Measurement

Molecular weight is reported casually in most student surveys of biopolymers — "high molecular weight" — but the distribution, not just the average, is where the biology shows through, and it is where this review earns its keep as more than a materials catalogue.

**Definitions.** Number-average (Mn) and weight-average (Mw) molecular weight, dispersity Đ = Mw/Mn (formerly "PDI"), and degree of polymerisation (DP) are the standard descriptors; Đ matters because it, not Mn alone, determines processing behaviour and the breadth of the mechanical property distribution within a single batch.

**Biosynthesis produces distinctive, family-specific distributions**, and this is the section's central point: dispersity is not noise, it is a direct readout of synthesis mechanism.

- Proteins and nucleic acids are synthesised under template control (ribosome, polymerase): every molecule of a given protein has identical sequence and length, giving Đ = 1.0 exactly — a monodispersity no synthetic polymer achieves outside careful living polymerisation.
- Polysaccharides are extended by non-templated enzymatic chain growth and are further fragmented during extraction, giving broad, source- and process-dependent Đ, commonly in the range of roughly 1.5 to well above 10 depending on material and isolation method.
- PHAs, produced by chain-transfer- and termination-limited enzymatic (synthase) polymerisation, typically show Đ ≈ 1.5–3.
- PLA's dispersity depends on how tightly the ring-opening polymerisation is controlled, ranging from near-living conditions to considerably broader distributions in less controlled industrial processes.

**Why molecular weight controls performance.** Mechanical integrity in the melt and solid state depends on chain entanglement above a characteristic entanglement molecular weight (Mc); below Mc, melt strength and toughness collapse. Above Mc, tensile strength typically rises with Mn and then saturates (a Flory-type relationship, σ ≈ σ∞ − K/Mn), so once a material is comfortably above its entanglement threshold, further increases in MW buy little additional strength. This saturation behaviour also explains why *degradation* — which manifests first and most sensitively as falling MW — can proceed for some time with only modest loss of mechanical performance before crossing back below Mc, at which point properties collapse abruptly rather than gracefully. This non-linear failure mode is directly relevant to service-life predictions in §6.3 and to drug-release kinetics in §7.2.

**Why measuring molecular weight is harder for biopolymers than for synthetic polymers** — this is the section's critical payload, and the reason MW deserves its own section rather than a parenthetical:

- *Solubility is the first obstacle.* Cellulose dissolves in none of the standard SEC eluents; measurement requires either derivatisation (e.g., tricarbanilation) or specialised solvent systems (cadoxen, cupriethylenediamine (CED), LiCl/DMAc), each of which can itself partially degrade or aggregate the sample.
- *Aggregation and branching distort size-exclusion chromatography.* Polysaccharides readily self-associate in solution, causing SEC columns calibrated against linear synthetic standards (polystyrene, pullulan, dextran) to over- or under-estimate true MW and to show anomalous tailing; branched architectures further decouple hydrodynamic volume from chain mass. SEC coupled to multi-angle light scattering (SEC-MALS), which measures absolute MW independent of column calibration, or intrinsic-viscosity methods using Mark–Houwink relationships are the more defensible alternatives — but Mark–Houwink parameters (K, a) are missing or mutually inconsistent for many biopolymer/solvent pairs in the literature.
- *MALDI-TOF mass discrimination* against higher-MW species further limits its use to lower-MW, narrowly dispersed fractions, which most native biopolymer samples are not.

**The resulting critical judgement, worth stating explicitly**: reported MW values for the same nominal material commonly differ by an order of magnitude across the literature, largely as an artefact of measurement method rather than genuine material variation. Any cross-study comparison of biopolymer molecular weight in this review — including the property comparisons in §6.2 — is only meaningful when the measurement method is reported and compared alongside the value itself.

### 6.1 Where biopolymers genuinely win

Four advantages are structural, not merely reputational, and each traces directly back to the structure–property material in §3:

- **Renewable, non-fossil carbon feedstock** — a supply-chain property, not a performance property, but the only advantage on this list that applies uniformly across all five families regardless of backbone chemistry.
- **Biodegradability in leakage-prone, single-use contexts** — agricultural mulch films, fishing gear, and disposable foodware are applications where end-of-life control cannot be guaranteed; here, the same hydrolytic/enzymatic susceptibility that is a processing liability in §6.2 becomes the desired property, provided the degradation environment matches the material (the material–environment pairing is addressed rigorously in §6.3, not assumed here).
- **Biocompatibility and bioresorbability** — PLA/PGA sutures and fixation devices, collagen and alginate hydrogels, and chitosan wound dressings exploit a property essentially unavailable in commodity synthetic polymers (PE, PP, PET) without extensive, costly surface or bulk modification. This is arguably the one application space (§7.2) where biopolymers are not a substitute for a synthetic incumbent but the only viable material class.
- **Intrinsic functionality beyond mechanical performance** — chirality (PLA stereocomplexation, §3.2), pH-responsive charge (chitosan), and sequence-encoded bioactivity (silk, collagen) are functionalities that arise for free from biological structure and that would require deliberate, often expensive synthetic chemistry to replicate in a conventional polymer.

### 6.2 Where they lose — stated with numbers, not adjectives

*(Figures below are illustrative order-of-magnitude ranges consistent with widely cited textbook/review values; each needs a specific literature citation before submission — flagged rather than invented precision.)*

- **Price.** Commodity PE/PP typically trade in the range of roughly $1–1.5/kg; PLA is commonly several times higher, and PHA — constrained by fermentation and downstream extraction costs — is typically the most expensive of the commercially available biopolymers. `[cite European Bioplastics / market report, as flagged in §8]`
- **Heat resistance.** Standard-grade PLA has a heat deflection temperature around 55–65 °C, well below PP's ~100 °C+, which directly excludes it from hot-fill packaging or dishwasher-safe applications without stereocomplexation or annealing (§3.2).
- **Barrier performance.** PLA and starch-based films generally show higher oxygen and water-vapour transmission rates than PET or EVOH, i.e. poorer barrier performance, which is why food-packaging applications of PLA typically require multilayer or coated structures (§7.1) rather than being used as a single-layer barrier film.
- **Brittleness and physical ageing.** PHB's secondary crystallisation (§3.2) and PLA's sub-Tg physical ageing both cause measurable embrittlement over storage time, independent of any chemical degradation.
- **Hygroscopicity and processing sensitivity.** Starch-based materials drift in properties with ambient humidity (§3.1); PLA must be rigorously dried before melt processing because residual moisture hydrolyses the ester backbone at processing temperatures, causing uncontrolled MW loss (§4) during extrusion itself.
- **Batch-to-batch variability.** Because polysaccharide and protein feedstocks are agricultural or biologically sourced, composition (e.g., alginate M/G ratio, chitosan DD, starch amylose/amylopectin ratio) varies by source and harvest in ways that synthetic, petrochemically-derived monomers do not — a variability that propagates directly into the structure–property relationships established in §3.
- **Recycling-stream incompatibility.** PLA is not compatible with existing PET mechanical-recycling infrastructure; even small PLA contamination degrades recycled PET (rPET) quality, making PLA a genuine complication for, rather than a simple addition to, current plastics recycling systems.
